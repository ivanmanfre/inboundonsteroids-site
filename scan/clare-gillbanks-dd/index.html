Reach UK founders who grow through events and introductions.

Written for business owners and BD leads who meet people at events and want those first hellos to turn into proper follow-up conversations.

Clare GillbanksUnlimited Connections - Click & Connect

The best connections usually start with one very simple line: "Hi, I don't think we've met."

I nearly didn't meet someone once, because I almost didn't go over and say hello. Most of us have stood at an event, comfortable with the people we know, wondering whether to cross the room.

Saying hello is the first step. What you do next decides whether the conversation goes anywhere.

Michelle Carr and I kept bumping into each other at London events in the property and construction space. We'd been meaning to sit down for a while. When we finally did, over breakfast, lots of new ideas for working together came out of one conversation.

If events aren't turning into proper conversations for you, these are the steps I'd look at:

  • Say hello without a pitch
  • Listen for one thing you can follow up on
  • Write a short note the same day
  • Suggest a proper 121 with a clear reason
  • Go into the 121 curious about their work

The slides walk through each step, with words you can use.

Sometimes it's just about making the time to sit down properly and talk.

If referrals bring you work, a small event is one of the kindest ways to say thank you.

I was at a lovely drinks reception in The Great Hall at Lincoln's Inn, hosted by Bishop & Sewell. Familiar contacts, a few new connections, and a very special setting.

What stayed with me was how well it was put together. It brought people into one room and acknowledged the value of the firm's trusted referrers, partners and wider network.

If you're thinking about doing something similar, here is what I'd plan for:

  • Invite the people who already send you work, plus a few they would enjoy meeting
  • Keep the room small enough that people can actually talk
  • Arrive early for a few quieter 1-to-1 conversations before it gets busy
  • Introduce guests to each other, so they leave with new names in their phone
  • Thank your referrers by name, in person, on the night

A beautiful venue helps. The introductions are what people remember.

Afterwards, send each referrer a short note naming who you introduced them to, and offer to make one more introduction if it would help.

Following up someone you met at a drinks event

01The example

Say you run an IT consultancy with a team of 10. You want to meet people who look after offices and buildings, so you go to an evening drinks event of about 50 people.

Near the bar you meet Sam, who manages facilities for a property company. You talk for a few minutes. Sam mentions an office move next year, and says nobody has decided who owns the IT side yet.

This use case follows that one conversation all the way to a proper 121.

02Before the event: decide who you want to meet

Write one line for yourself before you go. It keeps you focused without making you pushy.

Example: "I'd like to meet people who manage offices or buildings, to learn how they handle IT when a site moves or changes."

Then pick two opening questions:

  • What brought you here tonight?
  • What are you working on at the moment?

Leave the pitch at home. If someone asks what you do, give one plain sentence: "I help property and construction firms keep their IT secure and simple to run."

03In the room: open simply and listen for one detail

Open with "Hi, I don't think we've met." Then ask about them.

You are listening for one detail you can follow up on. In the example, it is Sam's office move and the open question of who owns the IT side.

Before you part, ask: "Would it be all right if I dropped you a line about that?" Swap details.

Before you leave the event, jot this in your phone next to Sam's name:

  • Where you met
  • What Sam is working on
  • Anyone Sam said they want to meet

04The same-day note, filled in

Send a short written note that evening or the next morning. Written is easier for Sam to read between meetings and find again later.

Example:

"Hi Sam, it was good to meet you at the drinks on Wednesday. You mentioned the office move next year and that nobody owns the IT side yet. I'd be glad to share the three questions I'd ask before choosing a supplier for a move like that. Would a coffee near your office work in the next couple of weeks? Tuesday or Thursday morning both suit me."

Why it works:

  • It names where you met
  • It repeats Sam's own detail
  • It offers something useful before asking for anything
  • It gives two clear times

05If Sam doesn't reply

People are busy. One gentle follow-up after a week is fine.

Example:

"Hi Sam, just bringing this back to the top of your inbox. Happy to send the three questions over by email if that's easier than a coffee."

If there is still no reply, leave it there and say hello at the next event. In a sector where you keep bumping into the same people, a warm hello in person picks the conversation back up.

06The 121: questions that lead to working together

Go in to understand Sam's world first. Useful questions:

  • How is the office move being planned at the moment?
  • Who else is involved in the decision?
  • What made the last move or system change hard?
  • Who would be a great introduction for you?

Close with one agreed next step on each side. For example: "I'll send over the three questions and a short outline by Friday, and you'll check who holds the IT budget."

Then do what you said, when you said you would.

The note to send the evening after an event

Clare Gillbanksto Alex

Hi Alex,

Thanks again for downloading the From Hello to 121 Playbook.

I wanted to pick out one detail from the first worked example, because it's the part that makes the rest work: the note you send the same day.

In the example, say you run an IT consultancy with a team of 10 and you meet Sam, who manages facilities for a property company. Sam mentions an office move next year and that nobody owns the IT side yet.

The note that evening does four things:

  • It says where you met
  • It repeats Sam's own detail, in Sam's words
  • It offers something useful first, in this case three questions to ask before choosing a supplier
  • It gives two clear times for a coffee

The second point matters most. When someone reads their own words back, they remember the conversation, and your note stands out from the other follow-ups in their inbox.

A small tip: jot that detail in your phone next to their name before you leave the event. It is much harder to recall the next morning.
