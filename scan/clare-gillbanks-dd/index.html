A good first chat turns into a partnership when someone books the second conversation.

Michelle Carr and I kept bumping into each other at London events in the property and construction space. We'd been meaning to sit down properly for a while.

When we finally made the time for a 121 over breakfast, lots of new ideas and ways to work together came out of it.

Sometimes it really is just about making the time to sit down and talk.

So if you met someone recently and thought “we should catch up”, here is how I'd move it on:

  • Write down what you talked about the same evening
  • Send a short note that mentions something they said
  • Suggest a 121 with a clear reason and two dates
  • Ask what a good introduction looks like for them
  • Make one introduction before you ask for one

The best opening line at a business event is “Hi, I don't think we've met.”

I nearly missed meeting someone once because I stayed where I was comfortable and almost didn't go over.

Most of us know the feeling. You recognise a few faces, you've found your spot, and someone across the room looks like they're thinking the same thing you are.

After a lot of years bringing people together, I've noticed the best connections rarely start with a rehearsed introduction.

A few things that make walking over easier:

  • Look for the person standing on their own or at the edge of a group
  • Say “Hi, I don't think we've met” and give your first name
  • Ask what brought them along
  • Leave the sales pitch out of the first few minutes
  • If the chat is going well, introduce them to someone else you know

That last one is my favourite part. A five-minute conversation can turn into something neither of you expected.

Next time you're standing there thinking “should I go over?”, go and say hello.

Hello to 121: a worked partnership follow-up

An IT consultancy follows up with an accountancy founder

01The setting and what to note that evening

For example, say you run a small IT consultancy that helps firms move to the cloud and tighten cyber security. At a London drinks evening you chat to Sam, who runs an accountancy practice for growing businesses.

Before the evening is over, write down:

  • What Sam does: outsourced finance for firms that have outgrown their bookkeeper
  • Who Sam wants to meet: founders whose business is growing faster than its systems
  • Something personal: Sam has just come back from a family trip to Cornwall
  • Why it could work: you both tend to get called in when a business is growing faster than its systems

These four lines are all you need to write a follow-up that sounds like you were listening.

02The follow-up message

Send this within a day or two, by LinkedIn or email.

Hi Sam, it was really good to meet you at the drinks on Wednesday. I hope the Cornwall glow hasn't worn off yet.

You mentioned that a lot of your clients call you once their finance has fallen behind their growth. I see the same thing on the IT side, usually at the same moment.

Would you be up for a proper 121 over breakfast near your office? I'd love to understand who you work with best and share who I work with. Tuesday or Thursday next week both work for me.

Best,
Jo

The message mentions one personal detail, names the overlap between your businesses and gives two dates, so it is easy to say yes.

03The 121 agenda

Keep it relaxed but give it a simple shape.

  1. Hear the story of Sam's business
  2. Who Sam's best clients are and why
  3. What is happening when a client usually picks up the phone
  4. Who you each already know that the other should meet
  5. One next step each

Questions worth asking Sam:

  • What was going on in the business when your best recent clients first called you?
  • Who else is usually involved when you start work with a new client?
  • What does a bad fit look like for you?

Take notes. You will need them for the referral brief.

04The referral brief you swap

After the 121, write a short brief for each other. Here is Sam's, filled in from the conversation.

Who I help: founders and finance leads of growing service businesses who have outgrown their bookkeeper.

Moments to listen for: “our numbers are always late”, “we've just opened a second office”, “the board wants proper reporting”.

Who to introduce me to: founders, operations directors, and advisers such as IT consultants, HR consultants and business coaches.

Not a fit: sole traders who only need a tax return.

And yours:

Who I help: owner-managed firms moving their systems to the cloud or tightening security.

Moments to listen for: “we've had a scare with a phishing email”, “our server is under someone's desk”, “a client has asked about ISO 27001”.

Who to introduce me to: founders, office managers and finance leads.

05The first introduction

Check with both people first, then send one short email.

Hi Sam and Priya,

As promised, here is the introduction.

Priya, Sam runs an accountancy practice for growing service businesses and is great at getting proper monthly reporting in place.

Sam, Priya runs a design studio that has just opened a second office, and she mentioned the numbers are always a month behind.

I'll leave you both to find a time. Happy to join the first call if it helps.

Jo

Each person gets one line on who the other is and why they should talk, using the moments from the brief.

06Keeping it going

A partnership grows through small, regular actions.

  • Put a check-in with Sam in the diary a few weeks after the 121
  • When you pass on an introduction, let Sam know what happened next
  • Say thank you when an introduction comes your way, whatever the outcome
  • Invite Sam to an event where there are people worth meeting
  • Update your referral brief when your work changes, and send Sam the new version

The one question worth asking in your next 121

Clare Gillbanksto Alex

Hi Alex,

Thanks for asking for the Hello to 121 Follow-Up Guide. I hope it's useful.

There's one part worth trying at your very next 121. Ask the other person what is usually happening in a client's business when that client picks up the phone to them.

Most people answer “who do you work with?” with a job title or a sector, and that's hard to pass on. A moment is much easier to listen for.

In the first example in the guide, Sam, an accountancy founder, gives three moments: “our numbers are always late”, “we've just opened a second office” and “the board wants proper reporting”. Once you know those, you start hearing them in conversations with your own clients and contacts.

Write the moments into the referral brief straight after the 121. Then send the other person yours as well, so you're both listening out for each other.

If you'd like to talk through who you're hoping to meet, just reply to this email.

Best wishes,
Clare
