How to write a creator brief for a music launch

Written for marketing leads and founders at music gear and music software brands who are getting ready to brief creators for a launch. It helps them set one goal, choose fitting creators and agree on rights before anyone films.

Brands often tell us: "We sent out free gear but saw no content."

When that happens, the brief is a good place to look first. A creator who gets a box and no direction has to guess what you want, and a guess rarely turns into a video that sells.

The slides walk through the parts of a creator brief for a music gear or music software launch:

- one goal you can trace to a sale - creators who already play in the culture - three talking points and no script - what the creator gets and what you need back - usage rights before anyone films - a fact-only review - tracking set up before the first post

I also wrote two full briefs as worked examples, one for a guitar pedal launch and one for a plugin launch. You can copy the shape and swap in your own product.

Which part of your brief is hardest to write right now?

Suno01 / 07

Start the brief with one goal a sale can be traced to

Pick one result for the launch and write it as a single sentence. Music brands often measure purchases, installs, trials, paid conversions or brand awareness. Each one needs a different brief. Example: say Northfield Audio is a small pedal maker launching a reverb pedal called Drift. The goal line reads: "Guitarists hear Drift in a real song and buy it on the Northfield store." Everything else in the brief serves that line. If an idea does not help it, leave it out.

It rained a lot in Nashville one stretch, and it got me thinking about Jetter.

The last months of 2024 and early January were calm for us. Then things picked up and started growing fast. Brands began to see the value of working with creators, and it was good to watch.

The same idea shows up in influencer marketing. When a brand moves from a few one-off partnerships to steady, long-term ones, that is when it starts to pour. The first results come quickly. The long-term fit with a brand takes longer and is worth more.

If you have run one creator deal and want it to turn into a steady partnership, here is a way to do it:

1. After the first post, read the comments with the creator. Write down the questions viewers asked. Those are your next video topics. 2. Offer the creator the next moment in your product calendar, like a new feature, an update or a bundle. Share the news early enough for them to plan a real video. 3. Agree on a rhythm that fits their channel, for example one post per product moment, and write it down. 4. Send what they ask for: audio files, demo units, straight answers about the product. 5. Look at results together and share what worked.

Musicians trust other musicians. A creator who keeps using your gear on camera builds that trust for you, one video at a time.

Creator Brief for a Music Product Launch

Example scope to send with your brief
Demo video
1 per creator: a full song or jam with Drift, posted on the creator's main platform.
Short clip
1 per creator: the best moment from the demo, cut for Reels, TikTok or Shorts.
Posting window
Both posted in launch week.
Link
Each creator gets their own link to the Drift page on the Northfield store.
Agree on the first review.

Northfield reads the draft for facts only: pedal name, price, ship date and any claim about what the pedal does. Tone, playing and editing stay with the creator.

Team develops→Client reviews→Team revises

Northfield may repost the demo and the short clip on its own channels. Running a creator post as a paid ad needs the creator's written yes, with the length of time stated.

Goal

Example brand: Northfield Audio, a small pedal maker. Goal line for every creator: "Guitarists hear Drift in a real song and buy it on the Northfield store." Measured result: purchases on the Northfield store.

Who we want

5 guitar creators who post ambient, indie or worship playing with reverb in most of their clips. Their viewers ask gear questions in the comments. We pass on creators who review a new product every day.

What we send

One Drift pedal to keep, this brief, and the payment terms agreed in writing before filming.

Talking points

1. The shimmer switch adds a high, airy tail to long chords. 2. The decay knob goes from a short room to a long hall. 3. It runs on a standard 9 volt pedalboard supply.

What the creator decides

The song, the guitar, the rest of the pedalboard and the words. Play something you already like. Use your own language and say what you would tell a friend who plays.

What we do not ask for

No read-out script. No discount code as the first line of the video. Please use the platform's paid partnership label so viewers know about the gift or payment.

Example scope. Adjust it to your project before sending.

Your creator brief: fill in the talking points first

ENElias Nolinto Alex

Hi,

Here is the creator brief document you asked for. Start with one part: the talking points.

In the Northfield Audio example, the Drift reverb pedal has three talking points:

1. The shimmer switch adds a high, airy tail to long chords. 2. The decay knob goes from a short room to a long hall. 3. It runs on a standard 9 volt pedalboard supply.

Each one is a single fact a viewer can hear or see in a video. The brief then lists what each of the 5 creators decides: the song, the setup and the words. Northfield only asks that the pedal name, price and ship date are correct.

Try it with your own product. Write three talking points. If a point needs a paragraph to explain, cut it down or leave it for the product page. Then write one sentence about what the creator decides.

Send both to your first creator with one question: "How would you show this to a friend who plays?" Their answer shows you how they would use your product on camera.

The Halo Master plugin brief in the document follows the same steps, so you can compare how it looks for software.
