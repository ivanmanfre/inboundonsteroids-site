Reach church leaders rethinking their website for visitors.

Written for church staff who run the website and want to learn what first-time visitors are looking for, then answer them faster.

Elias NolinGreetyr

Church website numbers leave out the thing that matters most: what visitors came looking for.

Most church teams track the usual numbers. Visits, time on site, pages clicked, form submissions.

Those are worth watching. But none of them show intent.

A long visit could mean someone is reading closely. It could also mean they are lost, hunting for service times or trying to find out what happens with their kids.

So start with a simple question: why does your church have a website? The answer should not be because everyone else has one. It exists so people can find what they need and take steps towards your church.

That is why we built Greetyr. It hears what people are actually looking for, so your church can get them answers faster and with less friction.

You can start on this today without any tool. Write down the questions a first-time visitor brings to your site. Then check where each one gets answered and what step comes next.

The Visitor Question Map shows how, with two filled-in examples: a non-denominational church and a Catholic parish.

Your plan-a-visit page should answer the questions a nervous first-timer will not ask out loud.

Someone thinking about visiting will often check your site on their phone before they decide. They want to know what Sunday will feel like before they walk in.

Here is what I would put on that page:

  • Service times and how long a service runs
  • The address, where to park and which door to use
  • What happens with kids, from drop-off to pick-up
  • What people usually wear
  • Who will greet them and where to find that person

Then read the page as a stranger would. If a sentence only makes sense to members, rewrite it.

Hospitality starts before anyone reaches your parking lot. That is the reason we are building Greetyr in partnership with our founding churches, Love Church in San Diego and St. Anthony of Padua in The Woodlands.

The page does not need to be long. It needs to answer fast and end with one clear next step, like planning a visit or messaging someone on staff.

A good church website answers the questions visitors bring before they have to ask.

The Visitor Question Map is a worked document to help you do that.

It takes the questions people bring to a church site and lays out four things for each one:

  • The question, in the visitor's own words
  • Where the answer lives on your site today
  • A rewritten answer in plain language
  • One clear next step

It comes with two filled-in examples. One is a non-denominational church with two Sunday services and a kids ministry. The other is a Catholic parish, where visitors ask about Mass times, baptism, confession and whether they can come if they are not Catholic.

Use it with your team in a staff meeting. Pick the example closest to your church and swap in your own details. Then open your site on a phone and check each answer.

That is what a church website is for: helping people find what they need and take a step towards your church.

Example: a church with two Sunday services and a kids ministry

01How to read this example

Say your church meets on Sundays at 9am and 11am, has a kids ministry, and recently refreshed its website. This example follows four questions a first-time visitor brings to that site.

Each question has four parts:

  • The question, in the visitor's own words
  • Where the answer lives today
  • A rewritten answer in plain language
  • One next step

Copy the format, then swap in your own church's details.

02Question 1: When are services, and how long do they run?

Where it lives today: the homepage footer, in small text, with no mention of how long a service lasts.

Rewritten answer: We meet every Sunday at 9am and 11am. Each service runs about an hour and includes music, a message and time to meet people afterward. Both services are the same, so pick the one that fits your morning.

Next step: a Plan Your Visit button right under the times, so the visitor can tell you which service they are coming to.

Why it matters: visitors often look for this first. Put it near the top of the homepage and on the visit page, and keep it in the footer too.

03Question 2: What happens with my kids?

Where it lives today: a kids ministry page that describes the ministry's values but never says what happens on a Sunday.

Rewritten answer: Kids from birth to 5th grade have their own rooms during both the 9am and 11am services. When you arrive, head to the check-in desk inside the main entrance, on the left. You will get a matching tag, and a volunteer will come find you if your child needs you.

Next step: Plan your visit and a volunteer will meet you at check-in.

Why it matters: a parent is deciding whether they will feel safe leaving their child with strangers. Tell them where to go, what happens and how they will know their child is okay.

04Question 3: Where do I park, and which door do I use?

Where it lives today: only the street address, on the Contact page.

Rewritten answer: Our address and a map are below. Guest parking is marked with signs closest to the main entrance. Look for the doors with the welcome team outside. Someone there can walk you to kids check-in or help you find a seat.

Next step: a Get Directions link that opens the map app on the visitor's phone.

Why it matters: the first few minutes on site shape how the whole visit feels. A clear arrival plan makes it easier to walk in alone.

05Question 4: Can I talk to someone before I come?

Where it lives today: a general contact form that goes to the office inbox.

Rewritten answer: Yes. If you have a question about visiting, about faith, or about a hard season you are going through, someone on our staff would be glad to hear from you. Tell us a little about what you are looking for and we will get back to you.

Next step: a short message form with one question: what would help you most right now?

Why it matters: this is where you hear intent. What people type here tells you what they are really looking for, and your traffic numbers can't show you that.

06What to fix first

Open your own site on a phone and go through the four questions. Note how many taps each answer takes.

Start with the answer that is hardest to find. In this example, that is the kids answer, because the kids ministry page never says what happens on a Sunday.

Then check that every answer ends in one next step.

Keep a running list of new questions your staff hear on Sundays, by phone and by email. Add them to the map as they come in, using the same four parts.

How to write the kids answer on your church site

Hi Alex,

Thanks again for grabbing the Visitor Question Map.

One detail worth copying from the first example is how it answers the kids question.

Many church sites answer it with a ministry name. To a parent who has never visited, that name says very little. The question they actually have is: what happens to my child, and how will I know they are okay?

In the example, the answer covers three things in plain words:

  • Where to go: the check-in desk is inside the main entrance, on the left.
  • What happens: kids from birth to 5th grade have their own rooms during both the 9am and 11am services.
  • How you know they are okay: you get a matching tag, and a volunteer will come find you if your child needs you.

Then it ends with one next step: plan your visit and a volunteer will meet you at check-in.

Try this on your own site this week. Open it on your phone, find your kids answer, and check whether a new parent would learn all three things.
