Reach agency founders turning sales calls into LinkedIn posts.

Written for service business founders who record calls, to help them find post material and reach buyers who could book a call.

Iván ManfrediManfredi (FreddyFlows)

Your sales calls already hold most of the posts you think you can't write.

An agency owner told me she had nothing to post about. She'd been staring at a blank composer for weeks.

So we opened her call recordings from last quarter. Three months of sales calls sat in a Fathom folder, transcribed and searchable.

Nobody had ever read them to write from.

On one call, a prospect said a freelancer would be cheaper. Her answer won the deal, and the buyer's exact words were right there next to it.

On another, she described the client's problem back to them more sharply than any headline she'd write from scratch.

We counted something like 30 to 40 posts worth of material.

The missing step was one pass over the transcripts to pull out the lines worth posting.

Here's how I'd run that pass:

  • Mark every objection and the answer that landed
  • Copy how the buyer describes their own problem
  • Save any moment a client says what changed
  • Turn each one into a post with a single point

Open your last three call recordings this week and run that pass before you write anything new.

Read the title of your lead magnet before you judge the download count.

A client showed me his latest one. The title was "AI for agencies." It had steady downloads and fed a Kit list of 10,000 free subscribers he was proud of.

So I sorted the list by who he could actually sell to. Under 2% of the names were in a segment he could work with. The rest were students and solopreneurs who'll never buy.

Before I look at any asset, I run the title through three questions:

  1. Who does it let in?
  2. Who does it turn away?
  3. Of the ones it lets in, what share could you close?

"AI for agencies" turns away nobody. That was the whole issue.

He changed one word to make it "AI for paid media agencies." He kept the same PDF and the same feed. ICP went up to 30 to 40% of the people downloading it.

A big list of the wrong people just gives you more names to sort through before you find anyone to talk to.

Before you publish your next resource, write the title, then name one type of reader it should turn away. If you can't, narrow it.

The Call-to-Post Worksheet

  1. The example call
  2. Moment 1: the objection, in the buyer's words
  3. Moment 2: the buyer describes the problem
  4. Moment 3: the question that shows how you think
  5. The post drafted from moment 1
  6. What to leave out of the post

Sales call: a buyer pushes back on price

01The example call

For example, say you run a paid media agency for ecommerce brands and record about 10 sales calls a month.

On one of them, the founder of a skincare brand likes your plan but stalls on price. The call runs 40 minutes. Most of it is discovery and small talk, but three moments in it are worth a post.

This part walks through those three moments, why each was picked and the post written from the first one.

02Moment 1: the objection, in the buyer's words

Transcript line (buyer):
"Honestly, a freelancer quoted us about half of this. Why would we pay agency rates for the same ads?"

Transcript line (you):
"A good freelancer can run the ads. The question is who covers creative testing, tracking and reporting when that one person is sick or busy with a bigger client in the middle of your launch."

Why keep it: this objection comes up on a lot of calls, and your answer already moved one deal forward. The buyer's phrase "the same ads" is how other buyers think about it too, so it makes a strong first line.

Tag it OBJECTION.

03Moment 2: the buyer describes the problem

Transcript line (buyer):
"We keep launching new products and the ads never catch up. By the time a campaign is ready, the stock is already moving or gone."

Why keep it: this is the problem in their own words, and it's sharper than a headline you'd write from a blank page. It works as the opening line for a post about planning campaigns before a product launch.

Tag it PROBLEM so you can find it later, next to other buyers saying the same thing.

04Moment 3: the question that shows how you think

Transcript line (you):
"Before we talk budget, which product are you launching next, and when does the stock land?"

Why keep it: it shows the order you work in. Readers with the same issue get a picture of how a call with you would go.

Tag it METHOD. A single line like this can carry a short post on what you ask first and why.

05The post drafted from moment 1

Draft post (example):

"A freelancer quoted us about half of this."

A skincare founder said that on a sales call, and it's a fair point.

A good freelancer can run your ads well. The real question is what happens when they're sick, on holiday or busy with a bigger client in the middle of your launch.

That's the gap an agency fee covers: creative testing, tracking and reports that keep going when one person can't.

If you're comparing quotes, ask each option who covers the work when the main person is out.

06What to leave out of the post

  • The buyer's or brand's name, unless they have agreed in writing
  • Exact prices, yours or the freelancer's
  • Any detail that could identify a deal that's still open
  • Small talk, scheduling and anything off topic

Keep the objection and your answer. Remove anything that would tell a competitor who was on the call.
