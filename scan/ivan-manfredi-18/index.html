When outbound stalls, find the stage where people stop before you change anything.

Outbound is a numbers game. I agree with that line. I use the numbers to find where people drop off.

Say you sent 100 connection requests and 20 accepted. The other 80 never got a message. They checked your profile and passed.

Rewriting your opener won't reach them. Look at who you targeted and what your profile says.

Warm signals help at this stage too. When I pulled my own invites, people who had seen me before the request accepted far more often than cold ones.

Accepts look fine but nobody replies? Look at the first message.

Replies come in but no calls? Open the threads. Check when the pitch showed up and whether those people could buy at all.

Then change ONE thing at that stage and leave the rest alone.

Change the list, the profile and the message in the same month, and next month's numbers move without telling you why.

Turning your own sales calls into posts

01Who this example is for

This part is for agency founders who still run their own sales calls and record them.

The example is invented to show the method. Say you run a paid media agency and have 10 recorded sales calls from last quarter, transcribed in a tool like Fathom. You have never opened the folder to write from.

By the end of this part you will have one pull note and one post draft from a single call, and a way to repeat it across the other nine.

02The four kinds of lines worth pulling

Read each transcript once, looking only for these:

  • An objection and the answer that moved the deal forward
  • The prospect's problem, said back to them in plain words
  • A client telling you what the work changed for them
  • A question more than one prospect asks

Skip small talk, pricing details and anything a client would not want public.

Copy the exact words. The buyer's phrasing is the part you cannot write from a blank page.

03Worked example: the transcript excerpt

From call 4 of the 10, with the founder of an online skincare brand:

Prospect: "Honestly, we could get a freelancer to run Meta for less."

You: "You could, and for some brands that's the right call. Before we touch the bidding, we look at the creative. Your last launches all spent well in the first week and then went flat. That pattern usually means tired creative, and someone running the account alone rarely has room to fix it."

Prospect: "Yeah, that's exactly what happened in spring."

That last line is your proof. The prospect confirmed your read of the problem in their own words.

04The pull note, filled in

Keep one note per usable moment. For the excerpt above:

  • Type: objection and answer
  • Their words: "We could get a freelancer to run Meta for less."
  • Your answer in one line: launches that go flat after week one usually point to tired creative.
  • Proof: the prospect said "that's exactly what happened in spring."
  • Post angle: cheaper account management won't fix a creative problem
  • Remove before posting: brand name, and the product category if it identifies them
  • Source: call 4, last quarter

05The finished post draft

A prospect told me they could get a freelancer to run Meta for less.

They could. So I asked about their last launches.

Each one spent well in the first week, then went flat.

That pattern usually comes from tired creative. Changing who manages the account won't touch it.

When I described it back, the answer was: "That's exactly what happened in spring."

Before comparing agency and freelancer prices, check whether your problem sits in the bidding or in the creative. The fix and the cost are different for each.

06Running the pass on all 10 calls

Work through the folder in order:

  1. Open one transcript and read it for the four kinds of lines.
  2. Write a pull note for every moment worth keeping.
  3. Tag each note with the call number so you can find the context later.
  4. Pick the strongest note and draft the post while the call is fresh in your head.
  5. Keep the other notes in one list for later weeks.

A call with a real objection and a clear answer often gives more than one post. The excerpt above could give a price post and a separate post on spotting tired creative.

One detail to use before anything else: open the call where the prospect pushed back.

In the guide's example, say you run a paid media agency with 10 recorded sales calls from last quarter. The first call to read is the one where a prospect said a freelancer could run Meta for less. An objection gives you three things in one place: the buyer's exact words, your answer, and usually a line that shows whether the answer landed.

To find these moments fast, search your transcripts for words like "cheaper", "freelancer", "in-house" or "not sure". Those words mark where a prospect was weighing you against another option. Your reply at that point is often the clearest version of why you work the way you do.

Fill in the pull note first: their words, your answer in one line, the proof, the post angle and what to remove. Then write the post from the note, not from memory.
