Most slow website work starts with a vague request.

For four or five years, Web Moves struggled to get work done efficiently. Data and tasks were scattered across my desktop, email, folders, notes and to-do lists.

I hit a breaking point and declared war on that mess. That is why we built My Site Buddy.

One thing became very clear along the way. "Fix the contact page" can mean the hours, the form, the phone number or the tracking. Whoever does the work has to guess.

A useful request covers five things:

  • What needs to change, in plain words
  • Why it matters to the business
  • What you already know about the page, form and tools
  • What the finished result should look like
  • Who says yes before it goes live

Write those down and the work, the preview and the approval can stay together.

I put together a worked website change request with two filled-in examples, one for a business owner and one for an agency team. Use it as a model for your next request.

Wins and losses in business both pass. Your discipline is what stays.

I have owned businesses for about 30 years, from a web agency to pest control and property management. The lesson I keep coming back to is simple: do not let the good months or the bad months swing you around.

A strong month can make you careless. A slow month can make you doubt what you know.

What helps me stay steady:

  • Know your data points before you react
  • Save hard when business is strong
  • Draw on those reserves when it slows down
  • Keep doing the work that brought the good months

For agency owners this shows up fast. One big client leaves, or a few new ones sign in the same week, and the pull is to change everything at once.

Look at the numbers first. Then decide.

Good luck and bad luck come and go, often faster than you expect. The owners who last stay even, stay consistent and keep showing up, whatever the market does.

Worked example: a plumbing company adds an emergency page

01How to use this worked request

This is a filled-in website change request for a business owner. Read it once, then copy the structure for your own change.

The example is invented. Say a local plumbing company wants a new emergency service page, and wants form leads that arrive after 6 PM sent to the on-call phone.

Each section below is written the way you would hand it to whoever does the work: your web person, your agency or a tool like My Site Buddy.

02The request in plain words

What needs to change:

  • Add a new page called Emergency Plumbing, linked from the main menu and a homepage banner.
  • The page explains what counts as an emergency, the service area and the number to call.
  • Add a short form on the page: name, phone, address and what is happening.
  • Form entries sent after 6 PM go to the on-call phone as a text message. Entries sent before 6 PM still go to the office inbox.

What should not change: the existing Services page, the current contact form and the office phone number.

03Why it matters and what we already know

Why it matters: people with a burst pipe at night land on the site, find only the office form and wait until morning. The owner wants those requests to reach the person on call.

What we already know:

  • The site runs on WordPress.
  • The current contact form sends to the office inbox.
  • The on-call person changes each week, and the owner keeps the rota in Google Drive.
  • Google Analytics and Google Tag Manager are already installed.

Open question for the person doing the work: should the on-call number come from the rota file, or will the owner update one setting each week?

04What the preview should show

Before anything goes live, the owner wants to see:

  • The new page on a preview link, on phone and desktop.
  • The menu item and the homepage banner in place.
  • A test form entry sent as if it were 7 PM, arriving on the on-call phone.
  • A test form entry sent as if it were 10 AM, arriving in the office inbox.
  • A form submission event showing in Google Analytics for both tests.

05Checks before it goes live

  • Page text read once out loud: does it say clearly when to call and when to use the form?
  • The phone number can be tapped to call on mobile.
  • The form shows a clear error if a required field is empty.
  • Nothing changed on the Services page or the current contact form.
  • Page title and description are written for people searching for emergency plumbing in the service area.

06Approval and the record

Who approves: the owner. Nothing goes live until the owner has seen the preview and both test entries.

What gets recorded with the change:

  • The original request, as written above.
  • What was changed, page by page.
  • The test results and who checked them.
  • The date it went live and who said yes.

If a problem shows up later, this record tells anyone what changed and why.

The line most website change requests leave out

John Wieberto Alex

Hi Alex,

Thanks for grabbing the worked website change request.

One detail worth copying from it: name the person who says yes, and say what they need to see before they say it.

In the dental clinic example, the agency looks after 20 client sites and the clinic's contact form stopped sending about 2 weeks ago. The fix has two approvals. The account lead approves the fix on the preview. The practice manager confirms that a test entry actually arrived in the front desk inbox.

That second check is the one that often gets skipped. A fix can look right on the preview and still send entries to the wrong inbox.

When you write your next request, add one line at the bottom, like this:

Approved by the account lead, after seeing a test entry arrive in the client's inbox.

It keeps the release decision with the right person, and it gives everyone a clear record of what was checked.
