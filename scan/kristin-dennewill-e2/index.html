A franchise network recovered 1,040 hours annually after we rebuilt their data architecture.

Before the migration, two development teams were running in separate systems. More than ten spreadsheets required manual updates every day to keep reporting alive. Corporate leadership had no view into unit, brand, or network performance without someone pulling the numbers by hand for each request.

After the migration, each team got up to four hours back in their day. What used to take a scheduled meeting to pull together runs as a live report.

The handover period was where the real work started. Years of reactive habit had shaped how the team spent their mornings. The routine was to start each day answering yesterday's questions, pulling the reports that someone upstream needed. The system no longer required it, and adjusting took longer than anyone expected.

A network heading into a third brand needs that recovered capacity pointed at the expansion work: which markets are ready, where the unit economics hold, and how the pipeline is building by location. None of that analysis runs well when the team is still buried in manual updates.

The network was months away from onboarding that third brand. The 1,040 hours they recovered in year one were the planning capacity they needed to make it work.

A marketer delivered 8x growth in average deal size and the company blew out its revenue number. He didn't get his bonus.

He missed his MQL target, which had been set at the start of the quarter before the deal structure changed.

That story stopped me cold. When activity metrics and revenue outcomes pull toward different things, teams optimize hard in the wrong direction and nobody notices until the bonus conversation happens.

In multi-location networks and PE-backed holding companies, this shows up at every level. Corporate sets targets in January. Each brand and each unit runs hard toward those targets. The deal structure shifts, the buyer profile changes, the network adds a brand, and the targets stay the same.

By the time the quarter closes, most goals are hit, and the aggregate picture still doesn't explain where the margin went.

A pipeline number and an MQL target should move together. When a new brand, a new buyer, or a shift in deal size changes what the business actually needs, the activity targets have to catch up. If they don't, you get a marketer who grew deal size 8x and still missed his bonus. The comp plan was measuring the quarter before the deal changed.

Day 1

Your CRM self-assessment, ready to run

The seven questions you just pulled aren't a pass/fail test. They're a map of where your revenue operations system is doing real work and where your team is covering the gaps by hand. Start with question three: if your top rep left tomorrow, what goes with them. That single answer usually shows how much of your pipeline lives outside the CRM entirely.

Day 5

The data input question that usually has no owner

Question four asks where consistent data entry breaks down and whose problem that makes it. The typical answer is shared ownership, which means it belongs to no one. When that's true, every pipeline report is a mix of clean fields and empty ones, and the forecast number your team presents is built partly on assumption. The assessment gives you the language to name exactly where the breakdown starts.

Day 12

If the questions surfaced something

If working through the assessment showed a gap in ownership, in reporting speed, or in what a new location operator would find in your CRM on day one, those are the specific things that stop the next growth phase.
