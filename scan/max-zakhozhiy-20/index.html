Written for ecommerce founders and marketing heads who want to check the margin behind their ROAS target before they scale spend.

Max ZakhozhiyUAATEAM

Before you set a ROAS target, check whether the margin you quote is only gross margin.

On an A/B testing call, a client and his internal team wanted to plan the next test. Before that, I asked some wider questions. Which channels bring customers? How strong is retention? How are photoshoots and creative handled? Then I asked about margins.

He said around 50 to 60%. My first thought was that this was excellent.

Then we went through the calculation. It covered production and shipping only. Paid media, agency fees, salaries, design and the redesign were all left out.

As gross margin, the number is fine. The trouble starts when it sets a ROAS target. Every cost left out still comes out of each order you pay to get.

Before you plan the next test or raise budget, write down four lines for an average order:

  • Gross margin: price minus product and shipping
  • Contribution before ads: also minus fees, packaging and returns
  • Contribution after ads: also minus ad cost per order
  • What is left for salaries, agency fees and tools

The slides go through each line and show how to turn it into a ROAS floor you can defend.

Traffic is rarely the first thing I would fix.

I recently sat in calls with two ecommerce clients. Both were sure traffic was holding them back. In both cases, the bottleneck was somewhere else.

More traffic is the easy lever. You add budget, campaigns and creatives. Each new customer still costs money.

The customers already in your database cost almost nothing to reach again. If nobody works with them on a schedule, the biggest growth lever in the store goes unused.

So now I start every strategy conversation with an audit of what is already running. Before any budget moves, check:

  • Repeat purchase rate for each monthly cohort
  • Welcome, post-purchase and win-back flows, and when each was last updated
  • Which channel first brought in your best repeat buyers
  • Where the cart and checkout lose people

Changing agencies, hiring freelancers or replacing the in-house team will not help if there is no marketing strategy underneath. That strategy starts with seeing what you have, how the parts connect and where they break.

Fix that first. Then scale.

From gross margin to a ROAS floor you can defend

01Why the margin you quote may be gross margin

Ask a founder for their margin and you often hear a strong number, like 50 or 60%. Usually that is price minus production and shipping. This is gross margin, and it is a useful number.

The problem starts when it is used to set a ROAS target or to decide on more budget. Paid media, returns, payment fees, agency fees, salaries, tools and design are all left out of it. Each of those costs still comes out of every order.

This document takes one order at a time from gross margin down to what the business keeps, using two example stores. It then shows how to turn that into a ROAS floor you can defend and how to fill in the same lines for your own store.

02The four lines to write down for every order

  1. Gross margin per order: order value minus product cost and shipping.
  2. Contribution before ads: gross margin minus payment fees, packaging and returns.
  3. Contribution after ads: contribution before ads minus the ad cost of getting that order.
  4. Left for fixed costs: contribution after ads times monthly orders, minus salaries, agency fees, tools and design.

Line 2 sets your break-even ROAS. Line 4 tells you whether the business makes money at the volume you run.

Use averages from the last 90 days. You can look at seasonal peaks and sale weeks separately later.

03Example 1: a home goods store with a $100 order

Say a home goods store has an average order of $100.

  • Product cost: $30
  • Shipping: $10
  • Gross margin: $60, or 60%
  • Payment fees and returns: $10
  • Contribution before ads: $50
  • Ad cost per new order: $25, a platform ROAS of 4.0
  • Contribution after ads: $25

Say the store takes 2,000 orders a month. Contribution after ads is 2,000 x $25 = $50,000. Salaries, agency fees, tools and design come to $40,000 a month, which leaves $10,000.

The 60% figure is accurate, but it tells you nothing about that $10,000. If ad cost per order rose from $25 to $30, the store would keep nothing at this volume.

04Example 2: a jewelry store with gift packaging and returns

Say a jewelry store has an average order of $200.

  • Product cost: $60
  • Gift box, packaging and shipping: $20
  • Gross margin: $120, or 60%
  • Payment fees and returns: $40
  • Contribution before ads: $80
  • Ad cost per new order: $50, a platform ROAS of 4.0
  • Contribution after ads: $30

Say the store takes 1,000 new orders a month from ads. That is 1,000 x $30 = $30,000. Fixed costs are also $30,000 a month, so new orders alone only break even.

Now add 250 repeat orders a month from email and SMS. These have no ad cost, so each one keeps the full $80. That adds $20,000, and it is the only profit in the month.

Both stores quote the same 60% gross margin and the same platform ROAS. Their floors are different, and in the second store retention carries all of the profit.

05Turn contribution into a ROAS floor

Break-even ROAS = average order value divided by contribution before ads.

  • Home goods: $100 / $50 = 2.0
  • Jewelry: $200 / $80 = 2.5

Below that ROAS, every new order loses money before any fixed cost is paid. At exactly that ROAS, new orders pay for themselves and nothing else.

To cover fixed costs as well, work out how much contribution after ads you need per order. In the home goods example, $40,000 of fixed costs over 2,000 orders is $20 per order. So ad cost per order can rise to $30 before the month's profit hits zero.

Use the floor to judge campaigns and to set Target ROAS in Google or Meta. A target based on gross margin alone will be set too low.

06What to change first when your ROAS sits near the floor

Before raising budget, check the lines above ads:

  • Returns: read the reasons. Sizing, fit and unclear product photos often show up here.
  • Shipping: show the full cost before the cart page so it does not cause late drop-off.
  • Average order value: bundles and gift sets make each order bigger without adding the same ad cost.
  • Repeat orders: welcome, post-purchase and win-back flows add orders with no ad cost, as the jewelry example shows.

Then move to ads. Pause campaigns that run below the floor, and shift budget to the ones clearly above it.

Scale only when contribution after ads is positive at the volume you want to reach.

07Fill this in for your own store

  1. Export the last 90 days of orders, refunds and ad spend.
  2. Work out your average order value.
  3. Take away product cost and shipping per order. This gives gross margin.
  4. Take away payment fees, packaging and returns per order. This gives contribution before ads and your break-even ROAS.
  5. Divide ad spend by new customer orders from your store data to get ad cost per new order.
  6. Split orders into new and repeat. Work out contribution for repeat orders without ad cost.
  7. Add up monthly salaries, agency fees, tools and design. Compare the total with your total contribution after ads.

If the result in step 7 is negative, more budget usually makes the gap bigger. Fix the lines above it first.

The line most margin math skips

Max Zakhozhiyto Alex

Hi Alex,

Thanks for downloading the margin document. I want to point out one line that changes the result more than people expect: returns.

In the jewelry example, the average order is $200. Payment fees and returns take $40 of it. Leave that line out and the store looks like it breaks even at a ROAS well under 2. Put it in, and contribution before ads is $80, so the floor is 2.5.

Here is a simple way to get your own returns figure:

  1. Export the last 90 days of refunds, including partial refunds.
  2. Add the return shipping and any products you could not resell.
  3. Divide the total by the number of orders in the same period.

This gives you a returns cost per order. Add it to line two of the document, next to payment fees and packaging.

If the figure looks high, check the return reasons before you touch the ad budget. Sizing, fit and unclear product photos come up often, and fixing them raises contribution on every order, including repeat orders.
