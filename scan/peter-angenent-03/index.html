A 4% rate assumption can hide much of a building's future water cost.

When I run 20-year ROI projections for greywater recycling, I have usually used a 4% yearly increase in water and sewer rates. I'm starting to think that's too conservative.

Across major development markets, recent and proposed increases are well above that. Phoenix has seen increases reaching 13%. Charlotte is around 6.5% for commercial and multifamily. Denver is around 5.5%.

Over 20 years, the gap compounds:

  • 4% a year ends at 2.19× today's bill
  • 6% ends at 3.21×
  • 8% ends at 4.66×
  • 10% ends at 6.73×

For a hotel, senior living community or multifamily building, that difference belongs in the proforma before the plumbing is designed.

The slides show how to run this test on your own project, and what to ask about onsite reuse once you see the numbers.

The last slide covers the full stress test document, with a hotel and a senior living example worked through from start to finish.

Every litre of drinking water a new building doesn't need is capacity a town can use for another home.

Municipal supply is measured in litres per second. When the Region of Waterloo forecast an increase of 20 L/s, I worked out what that means at the StatsCan average of 232 litres per person per day.

It's enough for about 7,440 more residents.

If each new build used onsite water reuse to cut its potable demand by 35%, the same 20 L/s would serve about 11,440 residents.

Where does that cut come from? In North America we flush toilets with drinking water. A low-flow toilet uses 1.6 gallons (6 litres) per flush, and each of us flushes about 5 times a day. That's 8 gallons (30 litres) per person, every day.

Toilets, laundry and irrigation can run on treated greywater from showers and baths.

For developers and planners waiting on water capacity, three questions for the next project meeting:

  • How much potable water does this building actually need?
  • Which uses could run on recycled water?
  • How much capacity would that free up for the next project?

Ask them before the plumbing drawings are done.

Stress test your water and sewer budget: hotel and senior living examples

01What this document helps you do

Use this before the plumbing is designed on a hotel, senior living or multifamily project.

It shows how today's water and sewer bill grows under four annual rate increases, what that adds up to over 20 years, and which uses in the building could run on recycled water instead of drinking water.

Two examples are worked through in full. Every figure in them is illustrative. Replace them with your own bill, your utility's published rate plan and your engineer's demand estimates.

02Step 1: Set today's water and sewer bill

Start with one number: the combined yearly water and sewer bill the building will pay in its first full year.

Where to find it:

  • Your utility's current water and sewer rate sheets
  • The mechanical engineer's design flow estimate
  • Bills from a similar building you already own or manage

If you have nothing yet, a rough demand check helps. Typical average daily use:

  • Hotel: about 209 gallons (791 litres) per occupied room
  • Senior living: about 109 gallons (413 litres) per resident
  • Single-family home: about 59 gallons (223 litres) per person

Multiply by occupied rooms or residents, then by your local combined water and sewer rate per gallon or litre.

03Step 2: Run four rate paths over 20 years

Multiply today's bill by these factors to see the yearly bill after 20 years of increases:

  • 4% a year: 2.19×
  • 6% a year: 3.21×
  • 8% a year: 4.66×
  • 10% a year: 6.73×

To get the total paid over 20 years, multiply today's bill by these factors:

  • 4% a year: about 29.8
  • 6% a year: about 36.8
  • 8% a year: about 45.8
  • 10% a year: about 57.3

Why test above 4%? Recent and proposed increases in several markets are higher. Phoenix has seen increases reaching 13%, Honolulu is at about 9% a year under its wastewater plan, Austin about 7 to 8%, Charlotte about 6.5% for commercial and multifamily, and Denver about 5.5%.

Check your own utility's published rate program. Run the path closest to it, plus one above.

04Worked example A: a 200-room hotel

Say a 200-room hotel pays $100,000 a year in combined water and sewer charges today.

Yearly bill after 20 years:

  • 4%: $219,000
  • 6%: $321,000
  • 8%: $466,000
  • 10%: $673,000

Total paid over 20 years:

  • 4%: about $3.0 million
  • 6%: about $3.7 million
  • 8%: about $4.6 million
  • 10%: about $5.7 million

Holding the bill flat would show $2.0 million. At 6%, the total is about $1.7 million higher.

Now say the design team estimates a 35% cut in potable demand from onsite greywater recycling, with a similar cut in wastewater. At 6%, the 20-year total drops from about $3.7 million to about $2.4 million.

The 35% is an example assumption. Use the figure your engineer or system supplier gives you for this building.

05Worked example B: a 150-suite senior living community

Say a 150-suite senior living community pays $50,000 a year in combined water and sewer charges today.

Yearly bill after 20 years:

  • 4%: $109,500
  • 6%: $160,500
  • 8%: $233,000
  • 10%: $336,500

Total paid over 20 years:

  • 4%: about $1.5 million
  • 6%: about $1.8 million
  • 8%: about $2.3 million
  • 10%: about $2.9 million

With the same example 35% cut in potable demand and wastewater, the 20-year total at 6% drops from about $1.8 million to about $1.2 million.

Senior living uses close to twice the water per person of a single-family home. Toilets and laundry don't need drinking water, so the reuse question is worth asking early, while the plumbing can still be laid out for it.

06Step 3: Find the uses that don't need drinking water

List each use in the building and mark whether it needs potable water:

  • Toilet flushing: no
  • Irrigation: no
  • Cooling towers: no
  • Laundry: often no, check local code
  • Showers, sinks and kitchens: yes, and showers and baths are the main greywater source

The uses marked no are what a greywater recycling or rainwater harvesting system could supply. The more of the building's demand falls there, the more the Step 2 numbers can move.

For LEED projects, LEED v5 includes a dedicated Water Reuse credit and expanded Enhanced Water Efficiency options, so this list also feeds the certification plan.

07Questions to take to your engineer and any system supplier

  • Is the system commercially certified, for example to NSF/ANSI and UL 979?
  • Was it designed for commercial buildings, or is it several residential units joined together?
  • Is it factory-built and ready to install?
  • How will it affect engineering, permitting and commissioning?
  • What long-term performance can it deliver, and what maintenance does that need?
  • What potable demand cut does it project for this building, and at what occupancy?

Put the answer to the last question back into Step 2 in place of the example 35%. That turns the stress test into one built on your own project.
