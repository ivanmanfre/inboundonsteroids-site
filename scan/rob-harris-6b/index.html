Reach B2B marketing leads planning a HubSpot website rebuild.

Written for marketing leaders on HubSpot who want visitors to trust what their site claims and take the next step.

Rob HarrisBetta Webs

When traffic is up and conversions are flat, look at your proof first.

People land on the page, look around and leave. Often it's because nothing there gave them a reason to believe you.

When we audit HubSpot sites for marketing teams, we keep finding the same thing. A case study says "40% growth". No client name attached. Nothing a stranger could check it against.

The result may be real. A buyer just has no way to check it, so they treat it as if it isn't.

The fix usually sits on the page you already have. Work through it claim by claim:

  • Find every claim a stranger can't check
  • Name who it happened to, with their permission
  • Say what changed, over what time and where it was measured
  • Put the proof next to the doubt it answers

The slides walk through each step.

The last slide covers the Proof Rewrite Worksheet: two worked examples, a SaaS homepage and a services case study, rewritten claim by claim.

Your pricing page can show you who is close to buying.

On our site, it's the second most-visited page after the homepage.

Any HubSpot account can track a visit to a page like that. It needs no special setup.

It only works if you have a pricing page for people to visit. Most agencies don't, so they can't see the visitors who are closest to deciding.

Someone reading your pricing is usually comparing you with someone else on their shortlist. Give that moment some attention:

  • Build a HubSpot list of contacts who viewed the pricing page
  • Check which pages they read just before it
  • Put real proof on the pricing page itself
  • Answer the questions a shortlist gets compared on

The same logic now applies to AI search. A chatbot recommending who to hire can only cite what it can find. No published price leaves it nothing concrete to point to.

We publish ours. Our site says most HubSpot website projects sit between mid four figures and low five figures, depending on scope, complexity and integrations.

If you can't publish a fixed price, publish a range and what moves it.

SaaS homepage: rewriting a results claim

01The starting point

Say a SaaS company sells booking software to dental clinics. The homepage hero says:

"Customers cut no-shows by 20%."

It sits under the headline, with a book a demo button next to it. There is no client name, no date and no link.

A careful buyer reads that line and has no way to check it. So they treat it as a guess, and the rest of the page picks up the same doubt.

02Step 1: Ask the four questions a buyer would ask

  • Who did this happen to?
  • What changed, from what to what?
  • Over what time?
  • Where was it measured?

In this example, the marketing team finds the answers in the customer success notes:

  • Who: Harbour Dental, a practice that has used the software for a year
  • What changed: missed appointments went from 100 a month to 80 a month
  • Time: the first six months after switching on text reminders
  • Measured: in Harbour Dental's own booking reports

03Step 2: Rewrite the claim so a stranger can check it

Before:
"Customers cut no-shows by 20%."

After, for the hero:
"Harbour Dental cut missed appointments from 100 to 80 a month in six months."

After, as the line under it:
"Measured in their booking reports after switching on text reminders. Read the full story."

The result is the same. Now a buyer can see who, when and how, and follow a link to read more.

04Step 3: Add the person behind the number

A result reads stronger with a named person next to it. Ask for one or two sentences in their own words.

Example quote to request:
"We used to spend Monday mornings chasing empty slots. Now the reminders do most of that." Sarah Lee, Practice Manager, Harbour Dental

Use a real photo if they agree. Skip stock photos of smiling people, because they make a real quote look less real.

05Step 4: Place the proof where the doubt appears

  • Hero: the rewritten claim, linked to the case study
  • Pricing section: Sarah's quote, because price is where buyers hesitate
  • Demo form: a short line on what happens after they submit, so the last step feels safe
  • Case study page: the full story, with the before and after numbers and how they were measured

Keep the same wording everywhere the result appears. If the hero says 100 to 80 a month, the case study says exactly that too.

06Step 5: The permission email to send the client

Subject: Can we use your results on our website?

Hi Sarah,

We'd like to share the change in missed appointments at Harbour Dental on our homepage. Here is the exact wording we'd use:

"Harbour Dental cut missed appointments from 100 to 80 a month in six months."

We'd also like to include your quote, with your name and role as Practice Manager. If anything should change, or you'd rather we didn't name the practice, just say and we'll adjust or remove it.

Thanks,
Jamie

Give every number on your site four parts

Rob Harristo Alex

Hi Alex,

One detail from the Proof Rewrite Worksheet is worth doing on its own, before you touch anything else.

Every number on your site needs four parts before a careful buyer will trust it: who it happened to, what changed, over what time, and where it was measured.

Take the first example in the worksheet. The homepage said "Customers cut no-shows by 20%." It may be a real result, but nobody reading it could check it.

The rewrite reads: "Harbour Dental cut missed appointments from 100 to 80 a month in six months." Under it, one line says the numbers came from their booking reports.

The result stayed the same. The team added the parts that were missing.

Try it on one number on your homepage. If you can't fill in all four parts, that's the claim to fix first.
