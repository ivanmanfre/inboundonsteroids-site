Anyone in marketing can build a landing page in an afternoon now. The checks that should happen before it goes live are easy to skip when the page goes from idea to published that fast.

Here is how I would group them before any page goes out.

Legal pages: privacy policy, terms and conditions, cookie policy, and a refund policy if anything is sold. Also ask whether cookie consent is needed at all, and which local laws apply rather than the ones the template assumed.

Forms and data: clear consent wording on every form, and only the data you truly need. Know what your analytics is tracking and what every third party embed on the page is doing.

Accessibility: alt text on images, colour contrast, forms that work from a keyboard, and button labels that say what they do.

Claims and content: no reviews you can't stand behind, no claims you can't back up, business details in the footer, and rights to every image.

None of it is hard. It only gets missed when nobody owns the list.

Proof rewrite guide: two worked examples

Proof rewrite guide: two worked examples

The claim as it stands

Say Harbor Ledger, a payroll software company for restaurant groups, has this line on its service page: "Our customer grew demo requests by 40%." There is no customer name, no time period and no link. The company is an invented example, used so every step below is filled in.

What a cautious buyer asks

Who is the customer? 40% of what number? Over what period? Who says so? Where can I see it? The page answers none of these, so the buyer treats the number as decoration. The number may be true. The page gives no way to tell.

Facts to gather from the client

Customer: Copper Table Group, a restaurant group with 12 sites (example). Starting point: 50 demo requests a quarter. After: 70 a quarter, two quarters after the pricing page was rewritten. Source: the customer's own form report, shared by Dana Reyes, marketing lead at Copper Table Group. Links: Dana's profile page, and a screenshot of the report with private fields hidden. Permission: Dana agrees in writing to be named and quoted.

The rewritten proof block

Headline: Copper Table Group went from 50 to 70 demo requests a quarter. Body: Dana Reyes, marketing lead at Copper Table Group, shared the numbers from the company's own form report. Before the pricing page was rewritten, the group received 50 demo requests a quarter. Two quarters after it went live, they received 70, which is 40% more. Quote: "Buyers arrive already knowing what we charge, so the first call starts further along." Dana Reyes, marketing lead, Copper Table Group. Links under the block: See Dana's profile. View the report screenshot. Read the full story.

If the customer can't be named

Ask first, using a message like this: "Can we name Copper Table Group on our site and quote you, with your job title and a link to your profile? You can review the exact wording before it goes live." If the answer is no, use a plain description: "A restaurant group with 12 sites (name withheld at their request) went from 50 to 70 demo requests a quarter." Keep the before and after numbers and the period. This is weaker than a named story, but it is honest and it still answers most of the buyer's questions.

Where the block goes

Place it directly under the first offer statement on the service page, so the buyer meets the proof while they are still deciding whether to keep reading. Link to it from the homepage and from any page that repeats the claim. Remove the old "40%" line everywhere it appears, so the page never shows the unchecked version beside the checked one.

How to ask a client to be named

RHRob Harristo Alex

Hi,

Thanks for taking the proof rewrite guide. Here is one detail from the first example that is worth doing before anything else: asking the client for permission.

A quick note first. The guide uses an invented example, a payroll software company called Harbor Ledger and its made-up customer Copper Table Group. The names and numbers below are illustrations, not real client results.

In the example, the site says a customer grew demo requests by 40%, with no name attached. The fix starts with one message to the customer. Keep it short and specific so it is easy to say yes to:

"Can we name Copper Table Group on our site and quote you, with your job title and a link to your profile? We would show the numbers as they came from your own report: demo requests went from 50 to 70 a quarter. You can review the exact wording before it goes live."

Three things make that message work.

1. It names what you will publish, so the client is not guessing. 2. It uses their numbers from their own report, so they can confirm them. In your case, swap in your client's real figures. 3. It offers a review of the wording, which removes the usual worry about being misquoted.

If the answer is no, the guide shows a fallback: describe them plainly, for example as a restaurant group with 12 sites (again, the invented example), and say the name is withheld at their request.

Once you have the answer, the rewritten proof block in the guide is ready to adapt. If you want a second pair of eyes, reply with your page and I'll tell you the first thing I'd fix.
