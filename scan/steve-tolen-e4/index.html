Reach recruitment leaders who want more BD conversations.

Written for recruitment founders and directors who want to turn their market experience into business conversations with the right clients.

Steve TolenStolen+Co

Your years in recruitment are the best reason for a buyer to take your call.

At a Calls That Convert workshop, it struck me again how often recruiters undervalue that experience. Ten or twenty years of talking to clients and candidates every day gives you a view of the market that most hiring managers never get.

Experts want to deal with experts.

So before you pick up the phone, ask yourself three things:

  1. What trend am I seeing in my market right now?
  2. How could it affect the person I want to talk to?
  3. What is my take on it, from everything I have seen?

Then open the call with that trend and your view on it. Ask how it looks from their side, and listen.

It won't guarantee a sale. It does give the other person a reason to talk, and that is where trust starts.

Your experience is an asset, so use it.

When a pipeline feels undercooked, look at activity first.

When my own pipeline feels that way, the cause is usually a lack of activity. Not the market, the algorithms or the tech stack.

One way to fix it is to split your week into two kinds of work.

Grunt work is the tasks that don't need a human touch. Research, list building, notes, scheduling. Use the tools for these and get them off your plate.

Ground work is the part only you can do. Pick up the phone, read the room, listen, and consult using your years in the market.

Here is a simple way to start this week:

  • List the grunt work tasks you did by hand last week
  • Pick two to hand over to a tool
  • Put the time you free up into calls with clients and candidates
  • Keep those call blocks in your diary like meetings

If you feel the phone is a little old-fashioned, you are at risk. The consultant part of your job title still means consulting, and that happens in conversations.

Example 1: A nursing agency calling an aged care group

01The example: a nursing agency calling an aged care group

For example, say you run a 10-person nursing agency in Sydney. You want a first conversation with the Director of Nursing at a mid-sized aged care group you have never worked with.

You have no job order and no referral. What you do have is years of talking to nurses and care managers every week. This plan turns that into a reason for the call.

Work through the steps in order. Steps 1 to 3 are thinking. Steps 4 to 7 are what you say and do.

02Steps 1 to 3: the trend, its impact and your take

Trend in one sentence:
Say you keep hearing from nurses that they want set rosters, and more of them are turning down short-notice agency shifts.

How it affects this buyer:
The Director of Nursing feels it when shifts go unfilled. If agency nurses decline short-notice work, the gaps land on the permanent team, which affects retention and care.

Your take:
From what you have seen, providers who book agency nurses in regular blocks, a few weeks ahead, get the same people back more often. Those nurses learn the residents and the site, and the permanent team trusts them.

03Step 4: the call opener, word for word

"Hi Sam, I run a nursing agency here in Sydney, and I speak with aged care nurses every week. One thing keeps coming up, and I'd value your view on it.

Nurses are telling us they want set rosters, and more of them are saying no to short-notice shifts. I'm curious whether you're seeing that on your side, and how it's landing with your permanent team."

Why this works:

  • It opens with something useful to them before any request.
  • It asks for their view, which invites a real conversation.
  • It is short enough to say before they decide whether to stay on the call.

04Step 5: questions to ask, and what to listen for

Ask:

  1. How are you covering short-notice gaps at the moment?
  2. What happens to your permanent team when a shift goes unfilled?
  3. If you could plan agency cover further ahead, what would change for you?

Listen for:

  • Words like burnout, overtime or resignations. These tell you the problem is costing them staff.
  • Whether rosters are planned centrally or site by site. This tells you who else is involved.
  • Any mention of a current supplier. Ask what works well with them first.

Share your take only after they have answered. Keep it to two or three sentences.

05Step 6: follow-up touches after the call

Plan the next touches before you hang up, so the first call has somewhere to go.

  1. Same day: a short message thanking them and repeating the one point they cared about most.
  2. Next week: share one useful thing, for example a note on how block booking can be set up, with no ask attached.
  3. Two weeks later: a comment or message on something they post, if it is relevant.
  4. A month later: a call to ask how rosters are tracking and whether a block booking trial is worth trying.

Each touch should add something. If you have nothing useful to say, wait until you do.

06Step 7: check cost, timing and value before you propose

Before you send any proposal, make sure you know the three things buyers weigh up.

Cost: How is agency cover paid for now, and who signs it off? You need to know how the decision is made, not a figure.

Timing: Is there a moment when this matters more, such as a new site opening or a roster review?

Value: What would a good result look like to them? Fewer gaps, the same nurses returning, less overtime for their team. Use their words.

If you can't answer all three, book another conversation before you propose.

The one line that makes the call plan work

Steve Tolento Alex

Hi Alex,

Thanks again for downloading the Market Take Call Plan.

One detail makes the biggest difference to the whole plan: the trend sentence in step 1.

A good trend sentence has two parts. Something you have heard more than once from clients or candidates, and who it affects.

In the tech recruiter example, say you run a boutique tech recruitment firm with 5 consultants. The sentence is that robotics roles ask for both deep software and hardware skills, and very few candidates have done both. It names the pattern, and it points straight at the Head of Engineering, who feels it when roles stay open and the product plan slips.

A weak version would be "the tech market is tight". Everyone says that, so it gives the buyer no reason to talk to you.

Try this before your next BD call:

  1. Write down three things you heard from candidates or clients this month.
  2. Circle the one you heard more than once.
  3. Add who it affects most, in one line.

That sentence becomes your opener. Your experience does the rest.
