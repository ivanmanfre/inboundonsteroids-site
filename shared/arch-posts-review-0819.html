For some mobile games, putting the whole budget on one influencer is the strategy that works. Say the budget is 100k. The default is to spread it across 20 creators, one video each, maximum reach. For some studios the opposite performs better. All 100k into one creator, multiple content pieces, a campaign with a story that builds from video to video. The audience gets repeated touches from a voice they already trust, and the creator has room to make something unique with the game instead of one brief-shaped integration. If usage rights are part of that deal, it's a win-win: the studio walks away with a library of ad creative on top of the campaign. Not every game fits this. But if your creative blends together across 20 channels, concentration is worth testing.

Your organic install number is probably lying to you, and it's flattering you while it does it. Here's the mechanic. A creator posts. Someone watches, doesn't tap the link, opens the store an hour later and searches your game by name. That install lands in organic. You look at the report, see organic climbing, and conclude your brand is finally compounding. So you cut creator spend, because organic is free and creators aren't. Then organic falls, and nobody can explain why. We put coupon codes and QR on campaigns for exactly this, so the install has somewhere to go that isn't the organic bucket. The codes only work if the creator gives people a real reason to use them: a freebie, or a discount that's actually attractive. Otherwise they search the store and the install disappears into organic. Do this before your next campaign. Write down your organic baseline for the 2 weeks before anything goes live. Then watch what organic does in the 2 weeks after. If that line tracks your creator calendar, those installs were theirs, and you've been undercounting the channel that was carrying you.

A broad-reach creator test gives you a hit or a zero. Almost never the middle. We've run enough of them on niche games to trust the pattern: a general-audience creator either performs like crazy or doesn't bring a single conversion. Niche creators are steadier - they're clustered around a middle you can forecast. That should change how the budget splits: → Broad creators get test money, sized so a flop changes nothing. → Niche creators carry the plan - you can call their numbers in advance. → When a broad one hits, scale into it fast. The split that follows: 20% of the budget on coin flips, 80% on outcomes you can predict. Averaging two coin flips into a CPA is how UA forecasts go wrong.

The cheapest CPM on your report is probably your worst buy. A creator's audience skews to markets with lower purchasing power. The CPM comes in beautiful. Everyone moves on happy. Then revenue per install arrives, and the campaign that won on paper starts losing. Banning those geos outright is a fair response, and plenty of studios do it. It's also throwing away players who pay fine - at the right price. The better lever: one CPM target per market, instead of one blended number for the whole world. If your target is $30 for example, the same creator can be worth $40 on a US-heavy audience and $8 on a low-purchasing-power one. Both are good buys at those prices. Both are terrible at the average. Where the per-market number comes from: revenue per install in that geo, against your margin. Price the audience you're actually getting.
