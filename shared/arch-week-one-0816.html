Two CPIs, one name, two different denominators

Two CPIs on the same slide, and the same word on both of them. One is a paid network's number. It counts the installs it can attribute to its own click, inside its own window. It also counts people who never clicked. TikTok's default gives a click seven days and a view one day. Google App campaigns count an engaged view, where somebody watched enough of the video and converted later. Meta took the view out of its default for new ad sets after ATT, so there it's click only until you change it. The other one is your creator line. A video sits in a feed with a link under it, so a click is the only thing there is to attribute. A player watches it, remembers the name, opens the store two days later and installs. Nobody clicked. That install lands in your organic row with no cost against it. Same word on both numbers, different populations underneath. Then there's the player who had the game before, deleted it, saw the video and came back. In AppsFlyer that's a reinstall inside your re-attribution window, and it isn't counted as a new install. Re-attribution needs a retargeting media source, and a creator UA link isn't one. Adjust is the same, one install per device unless its redownload rules fire. So the person the video brought back sits outside the number you divide creator spend by. Measuring a creator campaign on CPI is fine by me, and we're graded on it too. What I'd argue with is putting the two numbers beside each other as if they came off the same instrument. So what does yours actually say? Open your MMP, take the last period a creator line was live, and look at three things: - What your re-attribution window is set to - Whether view-through is on for the paid source you're comparing against - How many installs in that period were logged as reinstalls Two of those are settings somebody picked once. The third one is just a count.

Ten cuts off one shoot, still one person

An editor can get ten cuts out of one shoot by Friday. Hook swaps, a new endcard, a six second version, a static pulled off one frame. Real work, and cheap. We ask for it constantly. On the creator's own feed those cuts land in front of the people who follow that creator. Run the same asset as a Spark Ad and the targeting is yours, so the audience behind the face becomes whatever you bought. What no edit adds is another person. Another account, another set of people who trust it enough to install something. For that you book somebody, and the booking carries a date on their calendar. Match Masters ran across 1,500 influencers. That count caps how many genuinely different variants the campaign could carry. The edit list doesn't touch it. So check the last creator contract you signed. Where may that video run, in which ad account, in which market, for how long? If the paperwork doesn't say, some of those cuts are not yours to put spend behind.

Roblox guided its own bookings down, and said why

On 30 July Roblox told its shareholders that Q2 bookings grew 8 percent. Low end of its own guidance. Then it guided Q3 bookings down 14 to 18 percent. It also stopped giving a full year number, a quarter earlier than planned. Its first reason is a mix shift. Players moved off the high monetising viral games of 2025, onto newer and evergreen ones that earn less per hour. The second reason is the one worth reading twice. That mix shift, the letter says, was compounded by changes to the Recommended For You algorithm. The company started deploying them in April, to optimise for long term retention. The system now judges a player over 28 days instead of 7. It reads play through rate and first play bounce rate in that window, together with spend days and Robux spent per user. The letter is direct about the trade. Highly retentive games get shown to more players, and near term monetisation pays for it. On younger players in Q2 that ran bigger than the company expected. For Q3 it points at foundational changes across safety and discovery, so the age check rollout sits in that number too. Sponsored tiles are on sale through their ads manager, and Roblox has publicly tested putting sponsored experiences inside the Recommended For You sort itself. The ranking around them is the part no budget buys, and it's a 28 day read on how long somebody stays and what they spend. How much of your plan there assumes a sort order you don't set? Read the letter before you plan a quarter.

Take the uplift off the target

Paid creators make organic installs as well. Everybody agrees with that one! Then the ROAS target stays exactly where it was. There's a division for it. Pick a market with low organic volume, run the push there, and count the installs you did not buy. Post 06bench219 words

Gross or net, and net of which rate

A purchase that leaves a US app through an external link costs the developer nothing at the store today. It's been zero since the contempt ruling in the Epic case in April 2025. On 13 August Apple filed a proposal to put a number back on it. 15 percent on those external link purchases, 10 percent for the partner programmes and for subscription renewals, 5 percent for apps in the Small Business Program. Epic points at Apple's own filing, which concedes that a costs-only rate would be zero. The court has decided nothing. A booking agency reads a court filing because a ROI target only means something once you know which side of the store cut it sits on. Nobody pays a flat 30 anyway. There's the standard rate, 15 for a developer under a million dollars in proceeds in the Small Business Program, 15 on a subscription after the first year, and the EU sits on its own terms again. Apple's own subscription page then puts "minus applicable taxes" after all of it. So when a target arrives, the boring question goes first. Gross or net, and net of which rate? I'd ask it before the campaign, because the answer moves your break-even without anybody touching a creative.

A campaign underperformed. Which half?

A campaign underperformed. Which half of it? There are two conversion steps hiding under that word and they break for different reasons. Store page to install, then install to payer. If people arrive at the store page and don't install, the listing, the audience the video sat in front of and what the creative promised are where I'd look. That half is ours to move. Install to payer is where I'd like to tell you the rest is yours. Onboarding, the price, the offer, what the first session asks of somebody. It doesn't hold. Creative sets what a player expects before they ever open the game, and an over-promising video buys you a clean CPI and a cohort that never pays. That shows up in the second rate, on your side of the line I just drew, and the cause was ours. So here's the uncomfortable part, and I'll say it because an agency drawing this line is also drawing itself a shield. Weak creative can look exactly like a product problem from a dashboard, and the split I just gave you can't tell you which one you're holding. Retention between those two steps gets closer to it than either rate does. I don't have a clean way to settle that one.
