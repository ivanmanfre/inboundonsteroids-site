Creator fit check

Creator fit check

Use this when a marketing team has one or more creators in mind and needs a decision before outreach or booking. A good profile is a lead to investigate, not proof of audience fit or sales impact.

Ask for the decision inputs

  • Product, intended buyer or user, target countries, campaign goal and the task a viewer should see the product complete.
  • Creator handles or examples of recent relevant posts, their dates and links. Ask for audience geography, demographics and platform analytics if available.
  • Recent sponsored examples, sponsor overlap or category conflicts, quoted price, deliverables, usage rights and timing if known.
  • Any brand exclusions, access needs or measurement constraints.

Work with what the user has. Mark missing items Unknown; do not infer geography, audience composition, sponsored rates or performance from follower count or a creator bio. Do not browse or access private accounts unless tools and permission are available. If only a pasted description is supplied, say the assessment is based on that description.

Review the creator

  1. State the product's visible use case in one sentence. For a consumer or prosumer tool, prefer a result that can be understood quickly on screen. A selected B2B product may fit when the creator reaches the actual job role and can show a concrete task; do not reject it just because it is B2B.
  2. Review recent examples for topic relevance, demonstration style, audience discussion and consistency. Separate what a post shows from what the audience is assumed to be.
  3. Compare the creator's audience evidence with the target user and countries. A platform analytics screenshot is stronger evidence than a profile description.
  4. Check sponsored examples for disclosure, how naturally the product appears, competitor overlap, and whether the creator has actually explained similar tools. Ask for missing commercial terms instead of guessing them.
  5. Connect the creator to the campaign goal. For sign-ups, specify a trackable link or code and the attribution window. For awareness, name the reach and attention measures that can actually be supplied.

Return

  • Decision: Shortlist, hold for evidence, or pass, with a one-sentence reason. Never label a creator "approved" on the user's behalf.
  • Evidence table: Product demo, audience/geo, content relevance, sponsor history, economics/rights and measurement; for each, show Observed / Supplied / Unknown, the finding and its source.
  • Next questions: The few facts that could change the decision, especially audience geography and quoted sponsorship cost when absent.
  • Creator angle: One plausible 20-30 second demonstration idea, labelled a concept until the product and creator validate it.

Do not calculate CPM, CPA or expected conversions without matching costs and denominators. Do not treat an example of similar content as proof of campaign performance.

Creator brief writer

Use this after a product and creator have a plausible fit, or when a team needs a draft brief to discuss with candidates. Preserve creator voice while making the product demonstration and measurement clear.

Collect the brief inputs

Ask for the product link or approved fact sheet, target user and market, campaign objective, one demonstrable task and the result it produces. Also request platform, creator, format, deliverables, timeline, approved claims, proof or screenshots, required CTA, tracked link or code, review owner, disclosures and any restrictions. Ask about usage rights, paid amplification and exclusivity if those matter to the deal.

If a fact or term is missing, leave a labelled To confirm field. Never invent product capabilities, customer outcomes, legal claims, discount terms, creator fees or rights. A brief can be drafted with gaps; it cannot be treated as ready to send until the owner approves them.

Build the brief

  1. Open with the audience problem and one clear viewing outcome. State why this creator's audience might care, using supplied evidence only.
  2. Write a 20-30 second demonstration sequence: starting task, on-screen action, visible result and CTA. For a complex B2B tool, narrow the sequence to one recognisable job rather than trying to explain the whole platform.
  3. Give the creator room to choose language and filming style. Separate Must show or say from Creative options. Make each mandatory element attributable to a user-supplied requirement.
  4. List deliverables and operational details: format and length, draft date, review turnaround, live date, links or tags, disclosure, files to deliver, reporting date, rights and payment terms. Mark unknown terms To confirm.
  5. Pair the goal with a measurement plan. Specify the source for views, clicks, sign-ups or other outcomes, the window, and who collects it. Do not promise results.

Return

A one-page brief with these headings: Campaign objective; Audience and use case; The demo; Must include; Creative freedom; Deliverables and dates; CTA and tracking; Review and reporting; Terms to confirm. Finish with a short checklist of missing approvals that prevents this draft from being sent as a final brief.

Use straightforward language a creator can act on. Do not turn an unverified product claim into a script line.

First month creator test plan

Use this when a team wants to learn which creator and message combinations deserve more investment. This is a planning tool, not a forecast or a promise that a single month will establish lift.

Request inputs

Ask for product and target user, target countries, objective, campaign start date, available budget or spend ceiling, creator shortlist and quoted fees, production or review lead times, current conversion baseline, attribution setup, and any channel activity that could overlap. Ask which metric is the primary decision metric: qualified sign-ups, trials, purchases, or an awareness measure. If the user has no budget or baseline, plan the structure and show the missing decision inputs; do not invent a spend level or numerical target.

Design a useful test

  1. Write one main hypothesis in the form: "For [audience], [creator type or angle] will produce [observable outcome] because [reason]." Keep secondary questions small enough to interpret.
  2. Define test cells by creator type and message or demo angle. For a creator-profile comparison, hold platform, use case, offer and core message consistent where possible; vary the creator profile deliberately. If multiple dimensions vary, name the confounders and limit conclusions to directional observations rather than a clean comparison.
  3. Use the actual proposed dates, fees and deliverables to map a 30-day sequence: setup and tracking, brief and production, staggered launch, then reporting. If production cannot fit the month, say so and adjust the learning window rather than pretending it can.
  4. Specify tracking before launch: unique links or codes, landing page, analytics owner, reporting snapshot dates and attribution window. Record the baseline and concurrent campaigns where available.
  5. Set a decision rule for each cell before results arrive. Rules may use thresholds supplied by the buyer or relative evidence; do not manufacture "good" CPA or sample-size cutoffs. Mark a result inconclusive when the data is too thin, incomplete or confounded.

Return

  • A one-screen test plan with objective, primary metric, hypothesis, cells, measurement method and decision rule.
  • A week-by-week table with owner, dependency and deliverable.
  • A tracking table with creator, platform, angle, fee, link/code, launch date, spend, views, clicks, conversions and data source. Leave unknown values blank or To confirm.
  • Three possible decisions at readout: expand, revise and retest, or pause, each tied to observed evidence.

Do not convert views into sales or present correlation as causal lift. If the buyer wants incrementality, propose a separately designed holdout or controlled test and state that this plan alone cannot prove it.

Creator campaign readout

Use this after creator placements have run. Help a team decide what to repeat, change or stop without overstating what the data proves.

Ask for the data

Request goal and pre-agreed success criteria, date range, creator and placement list, fees plus production or amplification spend, platform views and engagement, tracked clicks, sign-ups or purchases, attribution window, landing-page analytics, baseline and concurrent activity. Ask for creator and campaign identifiers so the same person or conversion is not counted twice. If the user provides only partial data, read that data and list what is missing.

Analyse

  1. Reconcile the rows: placement, dates, platform, cost, reach or views, clicks, conversions and source. Distinguish missing data from zero.
  2. Calculate metrics only when numerator and denominator match. Examples: click-through rate = tracked clicks / views when the view definition is known; click-to-sign-up rate = attributed sign-ups / tracked clicks; cost per attributed sign-up = attributable campaign spend / attributed sign-ups. Show the formula and denominator. Do not calculate a ratio with zero or unknown denominator.
  3. Compare the results with the stated goal, baseline and pre-agreed decision rule. Group by relevant creator type or creative angle only when the sample supports that comparison. Note uneven flight dates, different offers, paid boosts and tracking gaps.
  4. Separate Observed, Possible explanation and Next test. An attributed sign-up is not necessarily incremental. Never call a campaign profitable without revenue and cost data that support that claim.

Return

  • Executive readout in three sentences: what ran, what the data shows, and the next decision.
  • Placement table: creator, angle, live date, spend, views, clicks, attributed outcomes and source or gap.
  • Metric table with calculation, source and caveat for each reported rate.
  • Decision table: Expand, revise and retest, pause, or inconclusive, with the evidence and the specific action needed.
  • A short list of the data to collect next time.

If the result is based on a few creators or a short window, call it directional. Do not invent platform analytics, conversion counts, audience geography or sponsorship costs.

EXAMPLE INPUT

An AI meeting notes tool for sales managers

Candidate: a YouTube creator who reviews AI productivity apps. Their public channel shows several note-taking reviews, including sponsored posts. No audience geography report or rate card has been supplied.

Question: “Should we shortlist this creator?”

EXAMPLE OUTPUT
Decision
Hold for evidence. The topic and demo format look plausible; audience and commercial fit are still unverified.
What we know
Recent review content suggests the creator can show a software workflow on screen.
What to ask
Audience countries and job roles, quoted fee, usage rights, sponsored performance and competitor restrictions.
Possible angle
Show a sales call becoming a usable follow-up note in one short sequence. Concept only; validate the product flow first.
