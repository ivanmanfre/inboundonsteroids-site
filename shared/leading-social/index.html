We recorded a podcast with Karl O'Brien a few months back and one of the topics on the table was why brands hate agencies, and where those relationships actually fall apart. I run an agency, a lot of my friends run agencies, and I told him I didn't get where the hate comes from. Karl's answer was the most useful thing I've heard on it. The agency is often the one putting the advertising spend to use, and he put that spend at anywhere between 10 and 40% of revenue. The founder is trying to talk about the financial side of the business. The agency, in his words, is "spitting marketing metrics back at them". I've been that agency. A few years ago a client of ours did about half a million in sales over Black Friday. When we reviewed it in January the margins were gone. We had no sight of their costs at that stage, and we had never asked for them. Karl mentioned brands that have gone through six or seven agencies in 24 months. His advice to them was "don't make the same mistake again". In his view, more times than not the relationship breaks down because marketing and finance have no shared understanding of the numbers. So if you're about to hire an agency, or replace one, I'd start with one question. Have they asked to see your costs? For years I thought my job was to improve ROAS. I came into every brand with ideas to push that number up, and in all fairness, pre-COVID most of us were in that boat. ROAS tells you how an ad performs today. CAC to LTV tells you whether the business can scale for years. So now the first thing we map for a new brand is LTV over time: day 0, day 30, day 60, day 90, day 180. Then we put the true CAC beside each one. True CAC means everything it took to get that customer: ad spend, creator fees, agency fees, content, the smaller tools running in the background. Let's say two brands both pay €40 to acquire a customer. The first gets €55 back on day 0, €75 by day 30 and €110 by day 90. Profitable on the first order, and that brand can usually scale hard. The second gets €30 on day 0 and €45 by day 90. Same CAC. That brand is bleeding cash while it waits for retention to save it, and there's no proof yet that it will. The rough healthy range we work to is around 1.5 times your CAC back within 30 to 60 days, and two to three times within 90 to 180. It won't hold for everyone. If you sell sofas, nobody is buying four a year. I broke the whole thing down in a video we now use as the SOP for every new client. I'll drop the link in the comments.

If you turned your ads off for the next 30 days, what happens to the business? Imagine two brands. Both do €100k a month. From the outside they look identical. Under the hood: Brand A gets 90% of that from new customers every month. Brand B gets 40% of it from customers coming back. Which one survives a bad ad month? Which one can scale without panic? Acquisition matters. Totally get that. It's mainly what I built my career on. It's how people find you, and you see the jump almost straight away. You launch ads, revenue moves, you feel the momentum. That momentum is fragile though. I've learned it in my own business too. It's strange to build a whole system around something you don't really control. Retention doesn't give you that adrenaline hit. A good email campaign is a nice morning. A new ad taking off within a couple of hours feels like you've got something going. But when customers start coming back on their own, a few things change. you stop rebuilding revenue from zero every month cash flow gets more predictable the ad account gets a lot less stressful and you usually have more cash in the bank It can be boring work. It probably builds the strongest businesses I see. You spent so much getting these customers. Make sure they're staying, make sure they're spending, then pair that with a strong acquisition plan.
